The research behind the question

Not a random Google stat on a slide — decades of experiments on possible selves, career regret, and how we make peace with what we let go. Tap a card.

01 · Theory tested

Your brain builds a “future you” — and it’s been tested

Markus & Nurius, 1986 Oyserman et al., 2006

The theory: Psychologists Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius proposed that everyone carries mental images of who they might become — a hoped-for self and a feared self — and that these images actively shape motivation, not just describe it.

The experiment: Daphna Oyserman’s School-to-Jobs intervention ran for six weeks with real students. Those who strengthened their “possible selves” showed measurably better academic behaviour and outcomes. A separate study with 42 adolescents (11–13) found occupational hoped-for selves were one of the most common themes kids generated unprompted.

Why this matters for us

The 8-year-old’s answer to “what do you want to be” isn’t a cute throwaway. It’s the same psychological system adults run on their whole lives — caught in an early, unfiltered state.

02 · Meta-analysis

Career is one of the top things humans regret

Roese & Summerville, 2005

The study: A meta-analysis pooled 11 separate regret-ranking studies. Across all of them, the same six life categories kept surfacing, in this order: education, career, romance, parenting, sense of self, leisure.

The follow-up: Lab experiments showed regret intensity tracked directly with lost opportunity — people hurt most over things they can’t undo, which is exactly the position career dreams end up in past a certain age.

Why this matters for us

When someone says they “just grew out of it,” the research suggests the pain of lost chance is real and measurable — not nostalgia for its own sake.

03 · Adaptive shift

Letting go might be the psychologically healthy move

Wrosch & Heckhausen, 2002

122 people aged 20–87 explained their biggest life regrets.

Younger adults who blamed themselves and still felt control felt less regret — “I can still fix this.”

Older adults showed the opposite: holding onto self-blame for something unfixable made regret worse. Attributing it outside their control — “it wasn’t really up to me” — linked to lower distress. A protective, adaptive shift once the opportunity is genuinely gone.

Why this matters for us

When an older adult says “there was nothing I could do,” that may be documented healthy coping — not denial. We reframe the interview from “do you regret it?” to: how did you make peace with it, and what did that peace cost?

05 · Our angle

What nobody’s actually asked yet

Cross-culture gap

Nearly all of the studies above were done in the US, UK, or similar Western contexts.

Nobody’s directly compared whether the “be realistic” pressure hits at the same age, or gets talked about the same way, across different cultures — and that’s exactly the angle our team can add, coming from different countries ourselves.

None of the existing research captures the texture of the moment either — where someone was standing, who said the sentence, what it felt like. Surveys give a percentage; a real interview gives the memory.

How this shapes our interviews

The 8-year-old: a live “possible self” — ask what might get in the way, even at 8.
The older adult: listen for “it wasn’t really my fault” as peace-making — and what that peace cost.
The expert: does protective letting-go look the same in Armenia, where family expectations may shape the “practical” choice differently?
